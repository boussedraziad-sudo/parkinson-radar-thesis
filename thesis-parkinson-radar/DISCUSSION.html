1What this chapter argues

The results chapter measured what this dataset supports under subject-independent validation: subject-level scores that sit in the same band as the score recording duration provides on its own. Two questions follow from that measurement, and this chapter answers both with experiments rather than argument. The first is why published radar gait studies report so much more. Section 2 reproduces the literature’s numbers on this very dataset by changing nothing but the validation design, first with the campaign’s own network and then with the field’s favourite architecture, and shows that those numbers collapse the moment a test subject is genuinely new. The second is what the scores obtained here are actually made of. Section 3 reads the models’ own attention maps, Section 4 deletes what they attend to and confirms that no hidden gait signature was underneath, Section 5 states the findings the data does establish, and Sections 6 and 7 close with the limitations and the concrete path to stronger results.

Key point

The framing the chapter defends. The gap between this thesis's numbers and the literature's is not a performance gap. It is a measurement gap: the two are answering different questions. The literature's question, can a model label recordings of people it has already seen, is answered here too, and with literature-grade numbers. The clinical question, can a model flag a person it has never seen, is the one this thesis insists on, and its honest answer on this dataset is: not yet, and not demonstrably from gait shape.

2The random-split demonstration: the gap is the protocol

The claim that the literature's numbers are inflated by validation design would normally rest on reading papers. Here it was tested: the same network and data as the main campaign, trained the field's way, with a random hold-out split, early stopping on window-level scores and no window weighting, so that the whole published recipe is reproduced rather than argued about.

Window-level random split (frame-level) 0.77 Nearly identical windows of the same walk on both sides of the split.
Recording-level random split (the literature’s hold-out) 0.76 Every test recording comes from a person the model trained on.

Leave-one-subject-out (the thesis protocol) 0.65 Every subject scored by a model that never saw them (subject-level AUC).
How to read the table

All four rows use the same architecture, the ResNet-18 transfer model of the main campaign; the first three rows are trained identically and differ only in what the split keeps apart. Row by row. The window split shuffles the three-second windows themselves (3 repeats), so nearly identical overlapping frames of the same walk sit on both sides of the split. The recording split shuffles whole walks (5 repeats), so each test walk is new but the walker's other walks are in training, which is the design most published studies use. In the third row, eight subjects (five control, three PD) are set aside before anything is trained; the model trains recording-split style on the remaining fifty subjects, and the finished model then classifies every window of the eight strangers' recordings; the score is over those windows (3 repeats, a fresh eight each time). In the last row, one subject is set aside, the model trains on the other 57, and then scores every window of all of that person's recordings (typically ten walks); the average of those window probabilities becomes that one person's score. Repeating this 58 times, once per subject, gives 58 scores, and the AUC over them is the number shown. One unit caveat: the first three rows are window-level AUC averaged over the repeats, the level at which the literature scores, while the last row is subject-level, one score per person. The trend is the point: the more the split leaks, the higher the number, and stripping the leakage returns the same model, on the same data, to the modest band the thesis reports.

Why this opens the discussion

Every later interpretation depends on this point. Once it is established that the protocol alone manufactures a 0.2 to 0.3 AUC difference on this very dataset, comparisons with published accuracies stop being embarrassing and start being diagnostic: the field’s numbers tell us more about its validation habits than about radar’s clinical value.

The same demonstration with the field’s own network

The demonstration above uses the thesis's own network, and a sceptic could object that the collapse says something about that particular model rather than about the protocol. So the experiment was repeated with AlexNet, the architecture behind the field's headline spectrogram result, adapted exactly as the thesis's ResNet transfer model: ImageNet-pretrained, first convolution averaged to the two radar channels, classifier retrained (54.5 million trainable parameters) at the source literature's learning rate. Same window cache, same augmentation; the random-split runs also adopt the literature's training conventions, early stopping on window-level scores and no window weighting.

A seed is one repeat of the split: the same pool of recordings shuffled with a different random starting point, so the three rows are three independent draws of the literature’s protocol. In the random split the units dealt into the 70/15/15 piles are whole recordings; in leave-one-subject-out the units are people, with 49 subjects training the model, 8 steering early stopping, and 1 being scored, rotated until each of the 58 has been the test subject once.

An AUC summarises how well the scores rank people; a screening decision is a yes or no. Two details make the honest AlexNet score itself instructive. Its subject scores carry more duration residue than any of the campaign’s headline models (correlation +0.25 with window count, against +0.08 for the ResNet transfer model): with 54.5 million parameters free, it drinks more deeply from the same confound, it does not find a new signal. And its best validation epoch averages 0.9, meaning the score is essentially in place after a single pass over the data, the learning profile of an easy shortcut rather than of a subtle clinical pattern.

3What the models actually read

The results chapter reported the scores; the attention analysis explains them. Grad-CAM maps were computed for every window of each of the 58 held-out subjects, for both image models: the compact CNN trained from scratch (SmallCNN) and the pretrained deep network (the ResNet-18 transfer model). One gallery per model follows, so the finding can be checked on each.

The pretrained deep network (ResNet-18 transfer model)

Three quantitative facts, computed over every window of all 58 held-out subjects, complete the picture. For the compact CNN, attention density is highest in the silent rows above 500 Hz (0.19 per pixel against 0.12 in the torso band), a region containing no gait energy, and its attention sits visibly higher in frequency on PD windows than on control windows (centre of mass 380 against 333 Hz). For the compact CNN, turn-straddling windows also draw four to six times the attention of steady-walking windows, matching the ablation in which excluding the turn collapsed its score. The transfer model differs in one telling way: because its backbone is frozen, its maps average out identical between the true classes (background attention share 0.337 on PD windows against 0.338 on control windows), so a fixed ImageNet description of these images contains nothing class-specific to point at, and its thin trained head can only read overall texture.

Key point

Taken together with the exploratory analysis, the mechanism of every score in this thesis is now explicit: elapsed time (carried by turn-and-stand content and window counts) plus per-recording character (carried by background and enhancement texture). Both are properties a screening tool must not rely on; neither is the spectral shape of a Parkinsonian walk.

4The intervention test: removing what they read

An interpretability finding invites an intervention: if the models read the background, delete the background and see what remains. The follow-up experiment, pre-registered before any window was rebuilt, subtracted each recording's background profile and cropped the silent rows beyond 500 Hz, then re-ran both image models under the unchanged protocol.

SmallCNN0.6160.624[0.471, 0.768]

Two conclusions, both informative:

  • No hidden gait signal surfaced. If the texture had been masking a real gait signature, removing it should have lifted the scores clearly above the duration floor. Every confidence interval still contains 0.610.
  • The nuisance is distributed, not localised. The scores did not collapse either: enough per-recording character survives inside the gait band, plausibly in the per-recording contrast enhancement, for the models to keep finding it. The recording-level nuisance is a property of how each file was processed, not one removable region of the image.

5What was found, stated positively

Not everything in this thesis is a negative result. The findings below are established by the data and hold under the strict protocol; each row gives the finding, the evidence in brief, and what it tells us.

FindingEvidenceWhat it tells us
PD subjects take measurably longer, and the extra time concentrates in standing up and turning. The duration gap between the groups is clear in the recording lengths, is largest in the walk variant that includes the chair phase, and shrinks when the turn is removed. Radar captures the slowness of movement that clinicians already test for; the timing of a walk is a real, usable signal.
Radar timing is a credible screening quantity. Recording duration alone separates the groups above chance under honest, subject-independent validation. An automated timed-up-and-go measurement is this dataset’s trustworthy first-line signal, and it needs no deep network at all.
A walk identifies the walker. Recordings of the same person are far more alike than recordings of different people, for every one of the 58 subjects. Subject-independent validation is mandatory, and radar gait could serve as a biometric even where diagnosis fails.
The field’s validation gap is real and measurable. Relaxing the protocol on this one dataset reproduces literature-grade numbers that collapse on unseen subjects. Published accuracies reflect validation design as much as clinical signal, so honest evaluation has to be demanded before numbers are compared.

Against the literature, these results are not an outlier; they are what the few honest data points predict. Of the sixteen works reviewed in the state of the art, only two report a genuinely subject-independent protocol, and one of them, the lightweight radar framework of Samimi Fard et al., saw its scores fall the moment it moved from a pooled split to leave-one-person-out evaluation. The study closest to ours in modality, the radar gait work of Hayashi and Saho et al., preferred its modest envelope model over a spectrogram network whose higher score its own authors distrusted, because that network had keyed on background differences between recording sites. This thesis extends that honest line to Parkinson’s screening and explains, mechanistically, where the distrusted scores come from.

6Limitations, and what would change the picture

LimitationConsequence, and what would resolve it
No age metadata. The dominant untestable confound: PD subjects are typically older, radar separates young from elderly walkers at 94.9 %, and even the surviving duration signal could partly be ageing. Subject ages would allow an age-matched analysis.
Combined foot channels hide left/right asymmetry. Asymmetry is an established PD marker, and it is structurally invisible after the two foot radars are merged into one channel. The original per-sensor recordings would make it measurable.
58 subjects. With 58 people, every score carries a wide uncertainty range, so a small real effect can neither be proven nor ruled out at this size. Only a much larger group, in the hundreds, can settle small differences.
No clinical information beyond the label. Each subject is only marked PD or control. Without disease stage, symptom severity or medication state, it is impossible to test whether the signal grows with severity, or how treatment changes it, both of which matter for a screening tool.

7Future work: the path to stronger results

The limitations above are not dead ends. Ordered from immediately actionable to long-term, this is how stronger and trustworthy results can be built on this line of work.

StepWhat it would deliver
1. Model the timing signature directly. Extract turn duration, sit-to-stand time and cadence from the spectrogram as explicit quantities, instead of hoping a network discovers them in pixels. Timing is the one signal this work showed to be real: patients take longer, and the extra time concentrates in standing up and turning. A model built directly on these timing quantities would be an automated version of the timed-up-and-go test that clinicians already use, which is also what the source radar system was validated to measure.
2. Obtain subject ages. An age-matched analysis would settle whether the surviving signal is disease or ageing, and could also unmask real signal that age variation currently hides.
3. Record a larger, session-balanced cohort. With 58 subjects every score carries a wide uncertainty range, so even a genuinely better model could not prove itself here. Demonstrating a spectral gait signature clearly above the duration floor needs a cohort in the low hundreds, recorded in sessions balanced across the two groups.
4. Keep the evaluation standard. Whatever the model, evaluate it on people it has never seen and report it next to the duration floor and a confidence interval. The protocol built for this thesis is pre-registered, ablation-tested and reusable as the design document for every step above.
Key point

The closing argument. This thesis set out to build a radar Parkinson’s classifier and found something more useful: a demonstration, on validated clinical hardware, of exactly how such classifiers come to look better than they are, and a protocol that prevents it. Every step above inherits that protocol, which is what makes the path forward credible: whatever signal richer data holds, the standard defended here will measure it honestly, on people the model has never seen.
