This chapter reviews the literature at the intersection of contactless radar sensing, micro-Doppler signal processing, and machine-learning gait analysis, and positions the present work within it. The survey is organised from the physical substrate upward: the case for radar as a gait sensor (Section 1); what a radar micro-Doppler signature physically encodes and how reliably it can be measured (Section 2); how deep learning has been applied to micro-Doppler spectrograms (Section 3); strategies for learning from the small datasets typical of this field (Section 4); the nascent use of radar for neurodegenerative disease (Section 5); and the validation pitfalls that recur throughout (Section 6). Section 7 distils the resulting research gap that this thesis addresses.

1Contactless gait sensing

Quantitative gait analysis is clinically valuable for detecting and monitoring movement disorders, but the dominant instrumented modalities each carry a cost. Wearable inertial sensors are accurate and inexpensive yet must be worn, constraining continuous use and biasing natural gait; optical motion capture and video are accurate but expensive, illumination-dependent, and privacy-invasive. Radar has emerged as a complementary alternative that is contactless, insensitive to lighting and clothing, and privacy-preserving, making it attractive for continuous and in-home monitoring [1, 2, 3]. These properties motivate the growing body of work that uses radar to characterise human gait, which the remainder of this chapter reviews.

Key point

Radar is contactless, lighting-independent and privacy-preserving — which is exactly why it is attractive for continuous in-home gait monitoring.

2Radar micro-Doppler foundations and gait-parameter validation

When a radar illuminates a walking person, different body parts moving at different radial velocities induce distinct Doppler shifts; the resulting time–frequency representation, the micro-Doppler signature, encodes the slow, steady motion of the torso at low Doppler frequencies and the faster swings of the limbs at higher frequencies [1]. Establishing what of this signature is physically trustworthy has been the subject of careful validation studies. Seifert et al. [1] benchmarked a 24 GHz Doppler radar against marker-based motion capture and instrumented-treadmill ground-reaction forces, showing that radar recovers spatiotemporal rhythm and pace parameters (stride and step time, cadence, stride length) reliably, but that joint-velocity estimates and gait-event timing are strongly dependent on geometry, viewing angle, and walking speed. Complementing this, the survey of Gurbuz et al. [2] catalogues radar-based gait-parameter estimation for fall-risk assessment and notes a crucial limitation: radar captures cadence and step-time well but measures gait variability—the stride-to-stride fluctuation that is a hallmark of parkinsonian gait—only poorly to moderately. The radar network that produced the dataset used in this thesis, López-Delgado et al. [4], sits in this validation tradition: it compares multi-node radar configurations against a Vicon reference and demonstrates accurate spatiotemporal parameter extraction in both healthy and Parkinson's populations, reporting that foot-oriented nodes best recover temporal parameters in motorically impaired subjects and that the torso-velocity–heel-strike coupling reliable in healthy gait breaks down in Parkinson's disease. Critically, however, that work stops at parameter measurement and contains no disease classifier—the point of departure for the present thesis.

Key point

Radar measures rhythm and pace reliably, but measures gait variability — a hallmark of parkinsonian gait — only poorly. The dataset paper for this thesis stops at parameter measurement and contains no classifier.

3Deep learning for micro-Doppler gait classification

A second line of work treats the micro-Doppler spectrogram as an image or a sequence and applies machine learning to classify gait. Two architectural families dominate. The first feeds the full time–frequency spectrogram to a convolutional neural network (CNN); the second extracts one-dimensional velocity time-series (for example the upper, lower, and power-weighted Doppler envelopes) and models them with recurrent networks. Hayashi/Saho et al. [5] exemplify the latter, classifying young versus elderly gait from 24 GHz micro-Doppler with a long short-term memory network on extracted envelopes, reaching 94.9% accuracy and reporting that the stance-phase (lower-envelope) feature alone was most discriminative. A companion simulation study [6] corroborates that leg-motion features carry most of the age-discriminative signal. CNN-based approaches on spectrograms are equally common across human-activity and gait recognition [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], with reported accuracies frequently in the 90–98% range. Notably, Papanastasiou et al. [7] and Ni and Huang [8] show that micro-Doppler encodes individual identity strongly enough to recognise people (>93.5% and 96.7% respectively)—a result that, as Section 6 argues, has direct and cautionary implications for how disease classifiers must be validated. Alternative front-ends have also been explored, including treating the Doppler signature with audio-processing pipelines [11] and low-cost millimetre-wave sensing for gait [3].

Key point

Two model families dominate: CNNs on spectrograms and RNNs on velocity envelopes. Crucially, micro-Doppler identifies individuals at >93% — which dictates how disease classifiers must be validated.

4Learning from small radar datasets

Radar gait datasets are typically small—tens of subjects—because data collection requires supervised sessions with specialised hardware. Two strategies recur for coping with data scarcity. The first is transfer learning: Seyfioğlu et al. [12] show that deep networks pre-trained on diversified micro-Doppler transfer effectively to new motion-classification tasks, and Park et al. [13] demonstrated transfer learning on micro-Doppler as early as 2016. The second is synthetic data augmentation: Erol et al. [14] use a kinematically sifted auxiliary-classifier GAN to synthesise realistic radar micro-Doppler signatures and expand small training sets. Both strategies are directly relevant to a 58-subject study and inform the transfer-learning and augmentation choices adopted in this thesis.

Key point

Transfer learning and GAN augmentation are the field's two standard answers to small radar datasets — both directly relevant to a 58-subject study.

5Radar for neurodegenerative disease and Parkinson's

Applying radar gait analysis specifically to neurodegenerative disease is recent and sparse. Cai et al.'s STRIDE system [15] uses radar intelligence for Alzheimer's-disease-and-related-dementia risk evaluation, illustrating radar's promise for passive cognitive-decline screening. For Parkinson's disease specifically, machine-learning gait classification has been pursued mainly through non-radar modalities such as force sensors and wearables [16], and the radar literature on Parkinson's remains largely confined to parameter validation [4] rather than automated diagnosis. To the best of our knowledge, no published work performs subject-independent Parkinson's-versus-control classification directly from radar micro-Doppler spectrograms—the specific gap this thesis targets.

Key point

Radar for neurodegenerative disease is sparse and recent. No published work does subject-independent Parkinson's-vs-control classification from micro-Doppler.

6Validation pitfalls: subject leakage, artifacts, and confounds

The high accuracies reported across the micro-Doppler classification literature must be read against a set of recurring methodological hazards, because several are known to inflate results. First, because micro-Doppler encodes individual identity so strongly [7, 8], any evaluation that allows recordings from the same subject to appear in both training and test sets permits the model to recognise people rather than the target condition; subject-independent (leave-one-subject-out) validation is therefore essential, yet much of the field relies on random hold-out splits [5, 6]. Second, spectrogram CNNs are prone to keying on nuisance artifacts rather than gait: Hayashi/Saho et al. [5] explicitly demonstrate that an AlexNet reaching 97.8% did so by exploiting background-noise differences arising because their two groups were recorded at different locations, not by learning gait—a textbook case of shortcut learning. Third, group-level confounds are pervasive. Age is especially insidious for a Parkinson's study: because micro-Doppler alone separates young from elderly walkers at 94.9% [5] and Parkinson's cohorts are typically older than controls, a classifier can appear to detect disease while in fact detecting age. Finally, the very features most diagnostic of parkinsonian gait—reduced velocity (bradykinesia) and increased stride-to-stride variability—are among those radar measures least reliably [2], so pipelines that aggressively normalise amplitude or use short temporal windows risk discarding the pathology they seek. Together these observations argue that methodological rigour—subject-independent validation, explicit confound control, and interpretability checks confirming the model attends to physiologically plausible Doppler bands—is the central unsolved problem in this niche, not raw accuracy.

Key point

The field's headline accuracies are undermined by three recurring failures: subject leakage, shortcut learning on artifacts, and uncontrolled confounds — above all age.

7Research gap and positioning of this thesis

The reviewed literature supports three conclusions. First, radar micro-Doppler is a validated, information-rich substrate for gait analysis, but the components of the signal that are trustworthy (rhythm, pace) differ from those most diagnostic of Parkinson's (variability, absolute velocity) [1, 2]. Second, deep learning on micro-Doppler is mature for coarse tasks such as activity recognition, person identification, and young-versus-elderly discrimination, but has not been applied to subject-independent Parkinson's classification [5, 7, 8, 4]. Third, the field's headline accuracies are repeatedly undermined by subject leakage, shortcut learning on recording artifacts, and uncontrolled confounds—above all age [5].

This thesis addresses precisely this gap. It performs, to our knowledge, the first subject-independent deep-learning classification of Parkinson's disease versus healthy controls from radar micro-Doppler gait spectrograms, using the multi-node FMCW dataset of [4]. Methodologically, it adopts the safeguards the literature identifies as necessary but rarely applies together: leave-one-subject-out cross-validation with subject-level score aggregation to eliminate identity leakage; fixed-length windowing to remove a trial-duration confound by construction; explicit duration-matched and age-matched control analyses to guard against the age confound highlighted by [5]; label-permutation testing and bootstrap confidence intervals for honest uncertainty reporting; and gradient-based attention checks to verify that the network attends to the foot and torso Doppler bands rather than artifacts. In doing so, the work aims to establish not merely whether Parkinson's gait is detectable from radar, but whether it is detectable under validation conditions strong enough to be trusted.

Key point

This thesis targets the gap: the first subject-independent PD classifier from radar micro-Doppler, with the confound controls the literature says are necessary but rarely applies together.

Doppler Radar for the Extraction of Biomechanical Parameters in Gait Analysis

Ann-Kathrin Seifert; Martin Grimmer; Abdelhak M. Zoubir (Signal Processing Group; Institute of Sports Science, Technische Universitat Darmstadt, Germany) · 2020

arXiv preprint, arXiv:2005.05280v1 [eess.SP], 11 May 2020

https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.05280

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

This is a signal-processing validation/measurement study, NOT a machine-learning classification study. Pipeline: CW radar return -> STFT spectrogram (Hamming window M=256, 255-sample overlap, zero-padded to 2048 points; ~40 Hz / 0.25 m/s frequency resolution, 0.1 s time resolution; data sampled at 12.8 kHz, sub-sampled to 2.56 kHz) -> adaptive noise thresholding -> morphological operations (dilation/erosion, hole filling, small-component removal) to extract micro-Doppler envelope signals. Novel contributions: (1) new methods to extract sagittal velocities of individual lower-limb joints (toe, ankle, knee) by using proportionally different noise thresholds (e.g., knee at 55% of adaptive threshold, ankle at 75-85%, toe at 85%), and (2) a new method to extract individual-leg flight (swing) time from radar by using the time instant of maximal knee velocity as a proxy for the toe-off event. Gait cycles segmented from envelope minima; 11 gait parameters extracted (stride/stance/flight/step time, cadence, stride/step length, maximal toe/ankle/knee velocity, and time instant of maximal knee velocity). Radar vs mocap agreement tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired, two-sided, alpha=0.05) across 220 tests, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction. Radar and mocap were time-synchronized via generalized cross-correlation of envelopes; a mean sampling-drift of 0.01 s/sample was corrected.

Key findings

Doppler radar can accurately measure many medically relevant gait parameters versus mocap+GRF. Five spatiotemporal parameters (stride time, step time, cadence, stride length, step length) were reliably extracted with near-zero differences across all speeds and both radar views. Three kinematic parameters (maximal toe, ankle, knee velocity) were accurately measured in most conditions, and radar captured the decrease in maximal knee velocity as the orthosis increasingly restricted the knee. Stance and flight time depend on correctly detecting the time instant of maximal knee velocity: this is reliably detected with a back view but underestimated with a front view (because the knee's signature is obscured by higher-energy upper-leg backscatter). Authors conclude radar is a viable contactless, privacy-preserving supplement for unobtrusive in-home gait analysis.

Reported performance

Kinematic velocities (toe/ankle/knee) measured by radar agreed with mocap in 51 of 60 analyzed cases (85%). Toe and ankle velocities were incorrectly extracted in 2 of 20 scenarios; knee velocity in 5 of 20. Kinematic median differences were < 0.3 m/s. For the flight-time method, the average difference between the time instant of maximal knee velocity and the GRF toe-off time ranged 13-40 ms; given mean flight times of 448 ms (0.7 m/s) and 402 ms (1.1 m/s), maximal expected flight-time error was ~9% (0.7 m/s) and ~8% (1.1 m/s). Benjamini-Hochberg significance threshold p* = 0.0057. Cadence, stride length, and step length matched almost exactly (e.g., Table II radar vs mocap cadence agree to ~0.01-0.02 steps/min). Stance/flight time reliable for all conditions at 0.7 m/s; at 1.1 m/s reliable with back view (except unconstrained gait) but difficult with front view.

Strengths

Contactless, privacy-preserving, unobtrusive, no user instrumentation - well suited for continuous in-home monitoring. Rigorously validated against gold-standard mocap AND GRF simultaneously (most prior radar-gait work only qualitatively compared). Introduces new, physically-motivated methods to extract individual lower-limb joint velocities and per-leg flight time from micro-Doppler. Demonstrates radar can sense small, induced changes in gait (orthosis), relevant to tracking relative disease progression. Sound statistics (Wilcoxon signed-rank + FDR control across 220 tests).

Limitations

Small sample (19), all young, able-bodied adults - no clinical or elderly population; gait abnormality only simulated via a knee orthosis rather than real pathology. Treadmill-only data, not real overground/in-home walking. Front-view radar underestimates the time instant of maximal knee velocity, degrading stance/flight-time accuracy; knee velocity obscured by higher-energy upper-leg backscatter. Envelope-extraction thresholds were tuned empirically and depend on subject stature, multipath, and transmit power. Authors state absolute values are "not sufficiently accurate for professional gait analysis (yet)." Only one axis of impairment (knee flexion) studied; asymmetry measures only suggested, not implemented.

Lesson for this thesis

(1) Radar micro-Doppler reliably captures spatiotemporal rhythm/pace parameters (stride/step time, cadence, stride/step length) but kinematic joint velocities and event timing are geometry-dependent - back view beats front view; this argues for controlling/reporting radar geometry and being cautious about knee-derived features. (2) Walking speed strongly affects the signatures and error rates, reinforcing that speed/duration are confounds to normalize (matches our duration-confound memory). (3) Validating on 19 young able-bodied treadmill subjects was explicitly flagged as insufficient - the authors call for "a larger clinical population," which supports the value of our 58-subject PD cohort. (4) They point to deep learning as the way to learn the micro-Doppler-to-gait-parameter mapping without hand-tuned thresholds, supporting our CNN over classical feature engineering. (5) Practical STFT settings (Hamming, heavy overlap, zero-padding) and the finer-than-intrinsic frequency grid mirror our own bin-spacing note.

Overview of Radar-Based Gait Parameter Estimation Techniques for Fall Risk Assessment

Sevgi Z. Gurbuz, Mohammad Mahbubur Rahman, Zahra Bassiri, Dario Martelli · 2024

IEEE Open Journal of Engineering in Medicine and Biology, Vol. 5, pp. 735-749

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11342925/

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

Review/survey paper combined with an original experimental pilot. Surveys radar-based quantitative gait analysis (QGA) methods for fall risk. Experimentally compares three data representations for estimating gait parameters: (1) micro-Doppler (uD) signatures, (2) RF skeleton reconstructed from real radar (SkelR), and (3) RF skeleton from simulated data (SkelS). A 3D CNN + bidirectional LSTM DNN reconstructs 14 skeletal joints (42 coordinates). Gait parameters analyzed: step time (ST), step length (SL), step time variability (STV), step length variability (SLV). Agreement with Vicon assessed via mean percent difference and Bland-Altman limits of agreement; RF-skeleton accuracy via joint MAE.

Key findings

Radar can estimate discrete gait parameters (step time, step length) with excellent agreement to optical motion capture, but gait variability measures (STV, SLV) — which are the most clinically informative for fall risk — show only poor-to-moderate agreement. RF-skeleton representations outperform raw micro-Doppler for step length (uD systematically underestimates SL by ~13%), while all methods do well on step time. Micro-Doppler captures whole-body aggregate backscatter (not per-joint), limiting fine spatial gait analysis. Deep-learning skeleton reconstruction introduces non-trivial estimation uncertainty from network design/training and is data-hungry.

Reported performance

Step time mean difference: uD 1.00% +/- 1.71%; SkelR -0.54% +/- 1.09%; SkelS -0.12% +/- 0.76% (all excellent agreement). Step length mean difference: uD -13.03% +/- 4.52% (good, underestimates); SkelR 1.84% +/- 1.05%; SkelS 0.90% +/- 0.72% (both excellent, ~1-2% error). RF-skeleton joint MAE (simulated): ML 3.15 cm, Vertical 4.37 cm, AP 1.4 cm; (real radar): ML 5.63 cm, Vertical 7.30 cm, AP 7.81 cm. Variability: STV poor-to-moderate (>50% limits of agreement), SLV moderate (10-50%). Cited lit: walking-speed validation error 0.076 m/s.

Strengths

Comprehensive, well-organized survey of radar QGA methods paired with a gold-standard (Vicon) validated pilot. Directly quantifies the accuracy gap between micro-Doppler and RF-skeleton representations. Highlights radar's non-intrusive, privacy-preserving, contactless nature suitable for continuous in-home monitoring without subject burden. Simulated-vs-real comparison usefully isolates DNN-induced uncertainty.

Limitations

Very small pilot (n=5, all healthy young males) — no fall-prone or clinical population, limiting external validity. Radar ran at only 10 Hz vs Vicon 100 Hz; poor elevation angular resolution (4 active elevation channels). Gait variability — the most fall-relevant metric — has poor/moderate agreement (up to ~100% overestimation). Micro-Doppler cannot isolate individual joints/body parts. Deep-learning methods are "data greedy" and carry estimation uncertainty. Multi-person disambiguation, obstacles, motion artifacts, and real-world clutter are not addressed; validation limited to controlled indoor lab. Kinect (often used as ground truth in cited work) is criticized as inadequate.

Lesson for this thesis

Gait variability/complexity features are the most clinically discriminative for gait pathology but are also the hardest to estimate reliably from radar — expect noise there, and be cautious about energy/duration-driven artifacts (mirrors our duration confound). Micro-Doppler = whole-body aggregate, so don't over-interpret features as joint-specific. Larger subject counts and rigorous subject-independent evaluation (our LOSO on 58) are exactly what this field's small pilots lack, strengthening our contribution. Consider positioning micro-Doppler CNN results against the known uD limitations (SL underestimation, variability instability) this paper documents.

Towards a Low-Cost Solution for Gait Analysis Using Millimeter Wave Sensor and Machine Learning

Mubarak A. Alanazi, Abdullah K. Alhazmi, Osama Alsattam, Kara Gnau, Meghan Brown, Shannon Thiel, Kurt Jackson, Vamsy P. Chodavarapu · 2022

Sensors (Basel, MDPI), Vol. 22, Issue 15, Article 5470

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9330716/

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

Signal processing: FMCW chirp processing, range/velocity FFT, Moving Target Indication (MTI) clutter removal, CFAR detection, DBSCAN clustering, and Kalman-filter subject tracking for multi-person separation. Machine learning: a four-layer CNN (kernel/filter depths 32, 64, 128, 256; Leaky ReLU, 2D max-pooling, batch normalization, 5% dropout, softmax) classifies micro-Doppler signatures into the five gait patterns; a separate two-layer CNN regresses 3D skeletal joint positions (19 joints) from the 5D point cloud. Five models trained and averaged to reduce system error. Emphasis on a low-cost, plug-in, real-time, privacy-preserving alternative to cameras and battery-limited wearables.

Key findings

The mmWave radar + CNN pipeline distinguished five gait patterns (normal, limping, stooped posture, walker, cane) with high accuracy and simultaneously reconstructed 3D skeletal pose in real time for multiple subjects. Assistive-device gaits (walker, cane) were classified most accurately, while overlapping gaits (normal, stooped, limping) were slightly harder to separate. Joint localization was accurate for large joints but degraded for wrist/hand joints due to radar range resolution. Authors position mmWave radar as a viable low-cost, contactless, privacy-preserving gait-monitoring solution for frailty/neurodegenerative-related abnormal gait.

Reported performance

Gait-pattern classification accuracy: walking with walker 98.8%, walking with cane 98.4%, normal walking 97.2%, stooped posture ~96.5%, limping ~95.7% (last two derived from the confusion matrix). Skeletal joint localization (mean over 19 joints): MAE = 5.86 cm (x), 2.98 cm (y), 5.49 cm (z); RMSE = 8.66 cm (x), 4.45 cm (y), 7.75 cm (z). Computational: total inference 2.2–3.7 s for 50,400 frames; per-frame 54.3–92.5 microseconds; power 3900.2–5763.4 mW.

Strengths

Uses low-cost, commercially available mmWave radar (contactless, works in low light, privacy-preserving vs. cameras, no battery burden vs. wearables); full pipeline from raw radar to both gait classification and 3D pose; real-time, multi-subject capability with explicit compute/power budgets; validated pose against Kinect V2 ground truth; high reported classification accuracy across five clinically motivated gait patterns.

Limitations

Gait patterns are simulated by healthy participants, not real patients — no actual Parkinson's/clinical cohort, so it is gait-pattern recognition rather than disease diagnosis. Validation protocol is not clearly specified: no explicit subject-independent or leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) split is reported and the train/val/test ratio is undisclosed, so the high accuracies likely reflect frame/sample-level splitting that risks subject leakage and optimistic bias. Higher localization error for wrist/hand joints (mmWave ~0.084 m range resolution); overlapping gaits (normal/stooped/limping) reduce class separability; lab-only data, limited generalizability.

Lesson for this thesis

Frame-level or unspecified splits produce very high (95–99%) accuracies that are not trustworthy for clinical generalization; our LOSO-on-58-subjects protocol is more honest and explains why our numbers look lower. When citing this paper, use it to justify the mmWave-radar-micro-Doppler-CNN approach and the clinical motivation (stooped/abnormal gait in PD), but explicitly contrast validation rigor: emphasize subject-independent evaluation and the duration/confound controls we apply. Also note their radar-resolution limits on fine joints as motivation for our Doppler-domain feature choices.

Utilization of Micro-Doppler Radar to Classify Gait Patterns of Young and Elderly Adults: An Approach Using a Long Short-Term Memory Network

Sora Hayashi, Kenshi Saho, Keitaro Shioiri, Masahiro Fujimoto, Masao Masugi · 2021

Sensors (Basel, Switzerland), MDPI — open access via PMC (PMC8197185)

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8197185/

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

24 GHz CW radar (40 mW EIRP; -3 dB beamwidth 70 deg E-plane / 28 deg H-plane; coherent detection). STFT (Hamming window, 128 samples) produces time-velocity (micro-Doppler) spectrograms. Instead of feeding full spectrograms, they extract three velocity time-series from each spectrogram: upper envelope v_u(t) (max velocity, swing phase), lower envelope v_l(t) (min velocity, stance phase), and power-weighted mean envelope v_m(t) (torso). Peak-extraction threshold rho = 0.2 for denoising. These 1-D time-series feed an LSTM classifier: sequence input -> 1 LSTM layer (400 hidden units) -> fully connected -> softmax (Young/Elderly). Adam optimizer, initial LR 0.0001, batch size 128, LR decayed x0.9 every 30 epochs, zero-padding for uniform batch length. Several input conditions tested (combinations of the three envelopes). Validation: hold-out 7:3 split (210 train / 90 test), average accuracy over 30 random trials — NOT subject-independent LOSO.

Key findings

Age-related gait differences (acceleration and stance-phase characteristics) are detectable from MDR leg-motion time-series. The lower envelope v_l(t) alone (stance/leg minimum velocity) gave the best classification, outperforming the upper and mean envelopes and their combinations. Feeding extracted envelope time-series to an LSTM captured comprehensive temporal information better than prior velocity-parameter/SVM methods. This is presented as the first radar-based method to exceed 90% accuracy on young/elderly gait classification.

Reported performance

Proposed LSTM with lower-envelope input: overall accuracy 94.9% (young 91.7%, elderly 97.8%). Baselines/comparisons: previous velocity-parameter SVM method 85.7%; CNN (AlexNet) on spectrograms 97.8% — but authors caution the CNN result is likely unreliable/biased because it keyed on background-noise artifacts (young vs elderly recorded at different locations) rather than gait.

Strengths

Contactless/remote sensing; no markers or wearables on the subject; unaffected by lighting and clothing; privacy-friendlier than video. Envelope-extraction preprocessing (peak threshold rho=0.2) suppresses background-noise artifacts, making the LSTM more robust than the raw-spectrogram CNN. Interpretable, physiologically grounded features (swing/stance/torso envelopes). Large sample (300) and strong reported accuracy (94.9%).

Limitations

Young and elderly data were collected in different locations, giving group-dependent noise characteristics — a location/site confound. The authors themselves show the AlexNet CNN exploited this background-noise bias rather than gait, casting doubt on spectrogram-CNN results. Validation is hold-out 7:3 (30 random splits), not subject-independent LOSO, so numbers may be optimistic and subject leakage is not ruled out. The mechanism for why the lower envelope works best is not fully explained. Dataset is not public. Future work suggested: middle-aged subjects, larger datasets, fuzzy time-series methods.

Lesson for this thesis

Their own CNN-vs-LSTM comparison is a cautionary tale: a spectrogram CNN hit 97.8% but was flagged as unreliable because it latched onto site/background-noise differences between groups rather than gait — exactly the confound-leakage failure mode we are guarding against. This supports (a) preferring interpretable, denoised gait-specific features (envelope time-series) or at least verifying our CNN attends to gait not artifacts, and (b) using subject-independent LOSO instead of random hold-out to avoid inflated accuracy. Also worth testing: extracting upper/lower/mean Doppler envelopes and feeding a sequence model (LSTM) as a baseline against our CNN, and checking whether a stance-phase (lower-envelope) feature carries most of the PD signal.

Reported performance

Test accuracies across 8 activity classes (Table II): TF-AlexNet 0.765 (76%), TF-VGG16 0.842 (84%), CVAE-DCNN 0.732 (73%), ACGAN-DCNN 0.825 (82%), PCA-ACGAN-DCNN tolerance 1.0 0.877 (87%), PCA-ACGAN-DCNN tolerance 0.5 0.932 (93%). Abstract: "An overall accuracy 93% is achieved ... with 9% improvement as a result of kinematic sifting." Fall detection 90%. Confusion matrix at 93% (Table III) per-class recall: bending 100, falling 90, gesture 96, kneeling 80, reaching 84, sitting 98, standing 98, walking 100 (%). Main confusions: bending<->kneeling and reaching<->sitting; 5% falling<->walking (short-stride walks resemble falls).

Strengths

Directly tackles the central radar-HAR bottleneck (small, costly datasets) with GAN synthesis rather than naive augmentation. Novel and generalizable contribution: PCA-based kinematic sifting injects physics/kinematic priors to filter implausible synthetic samples. Demonstrates genuine cross-domain generalization (train LOS lab data -> test through-the-wall, different room and unseen subject), a hard and realistic setting. Rigorous, multi-faceted evaluation (visual, kinematic consistency, diversity, latent space, saliency maps) and strong baselines (AlexNet, VGG16 transfer learning, CVAE, unsifted ACGAN). Published in a top venue (IEEE T-AES).

Limitations

The TWR test set comes from a SINGLE subject, so subject-level generalization is essentially untested and no cross-subject validation (e.g., LOSO) is performed. Population is healthy adults doing gross everyday activities (bending, falling, gesture, kneeling, reaching, sitting, standing, walking) — not gait-specific or a clinical/patient cohort, so no disease/pathology classification. Small and imbalanced real data (e.g., TWR kneeling n=15). Some class confusions persist (bending/kneeling, reaching/sitting, falling/walking). The reported 93% depends on aggressive sifting (tolerance 0.5); the sifting relies on PCA/tolerance heuristics rather than an explicit biomechanical model. Minor inconsistency: LOS/TWR location labels appear swapped between text and Table I.

Lesson for this thesis

Augmenting radar micro-Doppler data with GANs helps only when synthetic samples are constrained to be physically/kinematically plausible — naive ACGAN gave ~82% but kinematic sifting pushed it to 93%, and CVAE (blurry, low-diversity) actually hurt. If we use synthetic augmentation to expand our 58-subject PD set, we should add a physics/kinematics consistency filter. Their warning that visual similarity is not enough echoes our own duration-confound finding: a signature can look right yet carry a spurious cue, so features/samples must be validated against the underlying kinematics, not just appearance. Finally, their single-subject test set is a cautionary contrast that validates the value of our multi-subject LOSO design for credible subject-independent performance.

STRIDE: Systematic Radar Intelligence Analysis for ADRD Risk Evaluation with Gait Signature Simulation and Deep Learning

Fulin Cai, Abhidnya Patharkar, Teresa Wu, Fleming Y. M. Lure, Harry Chen, Victor C. Chen · 2023

IEEE Sensors Journal, vol. 23, no. 10, pp. 10998–11006 (open access via PMC; PMC10399976)

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10399976/

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

Pipeline: simulate gait -> generate micro-Doppler radar returns -> STFT spectrogram (512 DFT points, 256-sample overlap, 256-point Kaiser window; ~512 x 776 frequency-by-time spectrogram) -> deep-learning classification. Core method is a Band-Dependent Learning (BDL) framework: the spectrogram is split into N non-overlapping frequency sub-bands, each processed and assigned a learned importance weight (sigmoid-mapped to [0,1]) to down-weight noisy/uninformative bands, integrated on a ResNet-18 backbone (BDL-ResNet) yielding a 512-d feature vector into a 512x4 FC classifier (ReLU, batch norm). Training: Adam, lr 1e-3, batch size 64, cross-entropy loss, early stopping, up to 5 epochs. Evaluation: random 70%/15%/15% train/val/test split, repeated 30 times with re-splitting and re-initialization (NOT subject-independent / not LOSO — samples are simulated). Baselines: ResNet-18, GoogleNet, SVM, NN.

Key findings

Micro-Doppler radar plus a band-dependent deep classifier can distinguish four levels of ADRD-related gait abnormality on simulated data. The BDL-ResNet, which learns per-frequency-band importance weights, significantly outperforms standard CNNs and classical ML baselines. Best and worst separability are at the extremes (Normal, Severe) while the intermediate Subtle/Moderate classes are hardest. Authors frame abnormal gait as a non-cognitive biomarker detectable up to ~10 years before ADRD clinical symptoms.

Reported performance

Overall accuracy (mean +/- std over 30 runs): BDL-ResNet 0.923 +/- 0.018; GoogleNet 0.905 +/- 0.013; SVM 0.898 +/- 0.007; ResNet-18 0.884 +/- 0.078; NN 0.740 +/- 0.007. Per-class BDL-ResNet: Normal precision 0.977, sensitivity 0.996; Subtle precision 0.822, sensitivity 0.902; Moderate precision 0.920, sensitivity 0.794; Severe precision 0.995, sensitivity 0.997. Authors state "BDL-ResNet significantly outperforms the other algorithms in terms of overall accuracy with at least 95% confidence."

Strengths

Non-wearable and privacy-preserving relative to cameras and wearables. Introduces a novel, interpretable band-dependent weighting mechanism that reduces the impact of variability/noise across frequency bands and beats standard CNN and classical baselines. Digital-twin simulation allows controlled generation of graded gait-abnormality severity and large balanced datasets without recruiting patients. Repeated (30x) cross-validation gives variance estimates.

Limitations

Results are entirely on SIMULATED data — no real radar or human validation, so the high accuracy is optimistic and not directly comparable to real-world performance. Simulation is a near-empty room with only channel noise (unrealistic); lacks clutter and multiple subjects. Human walking model is not elderly-specific (no humpback/tremor). Evaluation uses random sample splits, NOT subject-independent (no LOSO), so generalization across individuals is untested. Intermediate severity classes (Subtle/Moderate) are hard to separate. Authors acknowledge need for field testing and transfer learning to adapt to real data. Targets ADRD/dementia gait severity, not Parkinson's specifically.

Lesson for this thesis

(1) Accuracy on simulated or subject-mixed data is not comparable to real-subject LOSO — cite STRIDE as the idealized ceiling and frame our lower LOSO AUC as the realistic, generalizable number. (2) The band-dependent weighting idea (learn per-frequency-sub-band importance, sigmoid-gated) is worth prototyping on our real micro-Doppler to suppress noisy bins. (3) Their own limitations (empty-room, no clutter, no elderly model, need for transfer learning and field testing) validate the value of our real-world dataset and motivate confound controls; also note the graded-severity framing (4 abnormality levels) as an alternative to binary PD-vs-control labeling.

Parkinson's Disease Diagnosis and Severity Assessment from Gait Signals via Bayesian-Optimized Deep Learning

Mehmet Meral (Dept. of Neurosurgery, Private Erciyes Hospital, Kayseri, Turkey); Ferdi Ozbilgin (Dept. of Electrical; Electronic Engineering, Giresun University, Turkey) · 2025

Diagnostics (MDPI, Basel), open access, Vol. 15, Issue 16, Article 2046. DOI: 10.3390/diagnostics15162046. Retrieved via PubMed Central…

https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/15/16/2046

Details, strengths & limitations
Method

End-to-end deep learning on raw VGRF signals, no manual feature engineering. Two architectures compared: a 1D CNN and an LSTM. Preprocessing = min-max normalization to [0,1]. Signals segmented into fixed-length windows of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 s. Hyperparameters tuned by Bayesian optimization (expected-improvement-plus acquisition, max 30 objective evaluations) using 5-fold cross-validation; search space included learning rate [1e-4, 1e-2], hidden units/filters [16-128], filter sizes [3-11], mini-batch size [16-128]. Final performance evaluated with 10-fold cross-validation stratified by diagnosis and HY stage. IMPORTANT: validation is segment/instance-level k-fold CV, described as subject-independent but NOT explicitly LOSO, and there is no external/independent-cohort test set — segments from one subject can plausibly land in both train and test folds.

Key findings

Bayesian-optimized deep models on raw VGRF achieve very high accuracy for both PD detection (binary) and HY severity staging (multi-class). LSTM consistently outperformed CNN, attributed to better temporal-pattern capture. Performance stayed >90% across all window lengths; Bayesian optimization added roughly 1-2% accuracy over non-optimized baselines. Best binary result at the 10 s window; best staging result at the 5 s window.

Reported performance

Binary PD-vs-control (LSTM, 10 s window): accuracy 99.42%, precision 99.42%, recall 99.42%, F1 99.42%, AUC 1.000. LSTM 5 s: 99.06% acc, AUC 1.000. Best CNN binary: 98.46% acc, AUC 0.998 (5 s). Multi-class HY staging (LSTM, 5 s): accuracy 98.24%, precision 97.47%, recall 98.25%, F1 97.85%, AUC 0.999; best CNN staging 96.62% acc, AUC 0.998 (5 s).

Strengths

Non-invasive and low-cost (instrumented insoles); end-to-end pipeline removes hand-crafted feature engineering; robust across multiple temporal window sizes; principled hyperparameter tuning via Bayesian optimization gives a measurable gain; reports a full metric suite (accuracy/precision/recall/F1/AUC) for both tasks rather than accuracy alone; uses a well-known public benchmark (PhysioNet gaitpdb) aiding comparability.

Limitations

Validated on a single public dataset with controlled recording conditions; the authors concede k-fold CV does not substitute for external validation on independent cohorts. No held-out subject-independent LOSO test is reported, so the near-ceiling (99%+, AUC 1.000) numbers are very likely optimistic due to possible per-subject segment leakage across folds. Severity classes are highly imbalanced (Stage 3 n=10), biasing multi-class results toward early stages. Only single-modality VGRF; real-world/free-living applicability untested. Future work: independent-cohort validation, hybrid CNN-LSTM, multi-sensor fusion, everyday-activity data, clinical validation.

Lesson for this thesis

Their near-perfect accuracy vs. our modest LOSO AUC is a protocol story, not a signal story: segment-level k-fold CV without subject partitioning inflates gait-PD performance because windows from the same person appear in both train and test. This directly validates our stricter LOSO design and confounds-control approach (duration-matched/residualized). Concretely: (1) cite this as an example of the leakage-prone evaluation we deliberately avoid; (2) borrow their good practices — Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and multi-window analysis (5-25 s) — which we could adopt for our radar CNN; (3) note their own admission that k-fold "does not substitute for external validation" to frame LOSO/subject-independent testing as the honest baseline for the field.
